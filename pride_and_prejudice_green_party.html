Pride and Prejudice: The Green Party’s Struggle with Policy

An examination of political consistency, manifesto commitments, and the limits of slogan-driven opposition.

Written by Maruchi White · The Loxfeld Strand

On the 29th of September, Zack Polanski, leader of the Green Party of England and Wales, tweeted the following on X:

“Why is the Government going after pensioners?
It’s time to tax the rich.”
(@ZackPolanski, 29 September, 2026)

This was in response to the announcement that Prime Minister Andy Burnham was seeking to replace the existing State Pension Triple Lock with an ‘adjusted State Pension’ system, under which the pension would rise in line with the average wage, rather than rapidly outstripping it. (Department for Work and Pensions 2026) The Green Party was not alone in their opposition. Most major parties in the UK spoke out against the proposed reform, and those that haven’t done so had committed to the system in their manifestos leading up to and since the 2024 General Election (Eaton 2026). Only one mainstream party, however, deviated from its 2024 manifesto: The Green Party. Their ‘Real Hope, Real Change’ manifesto ran with the following pledge:

“Elected Greens will campaign to:... Ensure that pensions are always uprated in line with inflation and keep pace with wage rises across the economy.”
(Green Party of England and Wales 2024, 19)

The proposed reform retains the existing 2.5% baseline and keeps the increase in line with inflation. What is now changing, from 2030, is the base ‘earnings’ criteria. Now, ‘the amount required to return or maintain the value of the State Pension relative to earnings’ will be the third pillar in the triple lock (Department for Work and Pensions 2026). The purpose of this, as is that of the Greens’ own proposed pension system, is to ensure that the pension rises fairly in line with the fiscal situation, without outstripping the average wage. Burnham’s reform is a step in the right direction, when placed against the Green pledge. Aside from the 2.5% baseline, the Greens have little to criticise. Indeed, in the tweet, Polanski offers little genuine grievance or alternative to the announcement, and reverts to the banal call of ‘tax the rich.’

A party that was serious about honouring its election promises might have criticised the reform for not being comprehensive enough, not having been properly considered, or for failing to align with its own vision of what reform should look like. The response should not be a resort to the formulaic ‘tax the rich’ reflex in response to every fiscal question. There is a genuine case for tax reform, and a conversation to be had about the distribution of the existing tax burden. It should not be reduced down to a slogan. This matters immensely, because the Green present themselves as a party of principle and honesty, in constant contrast to the establishment parties, whom the Greens describe as the primary drivers of ‘ripoff Britain.’ A party which claims that mantle must do its best to see its pledges as binding, and certainly cannot contradict it through pure reflex.

For me, the damaging aspect of this is that the tweet isn’t just an isolated lapse. The Green Party is obviously not without policy. They have a 50-page manifesto, and Polanski’s speech in March of 2026 to the New Economics Foundation clearly set out the Green Party’s economic agenda, with little change from 2024 (Green Party of England and Wales 2026 (a)). Like every piece of policy, it must face scrutiny, and any concerns must be duly addressed. In their assessment of the Greens’ 2024 manifesto, the Institute for Fiscal Studies stated that, even accounting for the optimistic £170 billion increase in tax revenue, their economic policies would require the Treasury to borrow £80 billion more to cover their additional planned spending (Emmerson and Miller 2024). At a time when the budget deficit was already about to reach £10 billion by December of 2024, these concerns needed to be addressed (Office for National Statistics 2024). Instead, Polanski responded by stating: ‘I reject this talk of holes. The household budget is nothing like a national economy,’ before talking about Labour, the Conservatives, and private banks (Torre 2025). None of this is entirely false, but a severe oversimplification, and certainly not a reassuring answer in an age of fiscal anxiety.

This reactiveness is endemic for the Greens. At the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War, Polanski called on the Government to set aside £8.4 billion to keep household bills down, funded by increasing taxes on businesses and wealth (Harris and Milligan 2026). Once more, the general idea of an emergency fiscal reserve is worthwhile, but the answer to every issue cannot always be to levy taxes on the same groups time and time again. The party also seems to have the habit of defining campaigns through an over-specific cause, sometimes relatively unrelated to the purpose of the campaign. Appealing to voters in Gordon and Denton to ‘punish Labour for Gaza’ does not necessarily suggest that the party is confident in its overarching policy platform (Miller 2026). It again boils down to a noble principle, and an important cause, being wielded like a slogan or catchphrase. This is populist politics, and unbecoming of a serious political party.

To be clear, this is not an issue unique to the Greens, and it would be unfair and disingenuous to suggest this. Every party deviates or outright ignores their election pledges, and your time would be better spent chasing this up for the party that actually governs your life. The issue with the Greens, however, is that, aside from a few flagship commitments or principles, I genuinely struggle to recall their policies in certain sectors. Personally, ideology aside, this makes it difficult for me to see them as a worthwhile party. Although their manifesto does set out their policies in varying degrees of detail, the party gives surprisingly little publicity to some of its names, such as the genuinely noble aim to have a qualified nurse in every school. A party must ensure that all of its aims are being pursued, both by its members and in Parliament, through pressure towards the existing government. Again, this is not to say the Greens alone suffer this issue. But empirically, it is my observation that there is a tendency to rely on it, particularly by Polanski himself.

That being said, opposition parties naturally exist to oppose, and to advocate for higher taxes on the wealthy in place of reforming the triple lock is not a baseless or invalid argument to make. It has been argued that the triple lock could theoretically be maintained in its current form by increasing taxes on the wealthy, either through a more progressive tax system or through capital gains tax reform, although a detailed analysis of these policies themselves is beyond the scope of this article (Murphy 2023). And within this, pledges themselves are aspirational, and not legally binding. Many may not be realistic, or may take many years to implement properly. But arguments must be made as arguments.

Polanski’s tweet neither engaged with the issue at hand nor referenced Green Party policy in any meaningful manner. Manifestos are useful for voters to track the party line and maintain accountability, and any deviations from them must be justified. The Greens have very rightfully criticised parties for not doing this, but a party that wishes for its policy to be trusted must be willing to cite it, examine varying proposals against it, and accept the result. But above all, it must be consistent. It must not be a case that the party discovers its policies when they point in the same direction as the polls.
